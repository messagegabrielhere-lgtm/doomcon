# Fear of the thinking machine

The cultural and intellectual record behind the joke, and the material the site
is allowed to build on top of it.

This is `TEARDOWN.md` §2.2 discharged. pizzint's history page sells the KGB
"PIZZINT" doctrine, Frank Meeks' 101 pizzas, Wolf Blitzer's line — and it is
the single reason their gimmick reads as a *tradition* rather than as a bit.
Ours can be better for one reason only: **all of our source material is real,
primary, dated, and checkable, and most of theirs is anecdote.** That advantage
evaporates the moment we ship one misattributed quote. So §8 of this file is a
misquote register before §9 is a quote bank, and every line in §9 carries the
exact venue and date a hostile reader would need to check it.

---

## 0. Rules for using this file

1. **Nothing here is a forecast, including the chapter titles.** That these
   people wrote these words on these dates is a fact. Whether they were right is
   not something this repo gets to assert. `VOICE.md` §1 governs.
2. **Describe and cite; do not reproduce.** A few words in quotation marks,
   attributed on the spot, in the same visual unit as the quote. Never a
   paragraph of someone else's prose.
3. **Date + attribution + venue, or cut it.** `history.mjs` already throws
   rather than render an entry that lost its citation in an edit. Anything
   promoted from this file inherits that rule.
4. **Confidence is marked.** Claims below are tagged **[verified]** (I read a
   primary text or a reproduction of it during this research pass),
   **[secondary]** (consistent across reference sources, no primary read), or
   **[inference]** (my reasoning, flagged as such). Never promote an
   **[inference]** to the page as a fact.
5. **The pre-flight applies to post copy, not to pages.** `VOICE.md` §3.3's
   carve-out — a quoted title of a cited work is reproduced verbatim even when
   it trips the word list — is load-bearing here, because four of the best lines
   in this file trip it. §9 marks exactly which, and it was measured, not
   guessed.

## 1. Ancient and pre-modern: the artificial servant that turns

### Talos — Apollonius of Rhodes, *Argonautica*, Book 4, 3rd century BC

The oldest surviving detailed account of an automaton in Western literature is
also a story about how one is killed. Talos is a bronze guardian who circles
Crete three times a day and pelts approaching ships with rocks. He has a single
vein running from neck to ankle, closed by a bronze nail. Medea works on him
from the deck of the *Argo*; he grazes his ankle, the ichor runs out, and the
bronze man falls. **[verified]**

Three things make this the right opening beat and not just decoration:

- The automaton is a **security system**, built by a god, operating a perimeter.
  It is not a companion, a curiosity or a labourer.
- It has **one vulnerability**, and the story is entirely about finding it.
- Nobody in the poem asks whether Talos has an interior life. The question the
  whole modern debate turns on is simply not posed.

> The oldest robot story in the canon is a story about a perimeter and a single
> point of failure. The genre started as an engineering problem and only later
> became a moral one.

### The golem — and the part of the legend that is much younger than advertised

"Golem" appears once in the Hebrew Bible, at Psalm 139:16, meaning an unformed
or embryonic thing. The Babylonian Talmud (Sanhedrin 65b) has Rava create a man
and send him to Rav Zeira, who speaks to him, gets no answer, and returns him to
dust — a creature that works but cannot talk. **[secondary]**

**The honesty beat, and it is a good one.** The famous version — Rabbi Judah
Loew ben Bezalel, the Maharal of Prague (d. 1609), animating a clay man to
protect the Jewish quarter, the creature growing dangerous, the name being
removed from its mouth — is **not a 16th-century story**. The attribution to the
Maharal first appears in print in Berthold Auerbach's 1837 novel *Spinoza*, more
than two centuries after his death. The rich, novelistic Prague golem most
people know comes from Yudl Rosenberg's *Nifle'ot Maharal* (Warsaw, 1909), which
presents itself as a found 17th-century manuscript and is Rosenberg's own
fiction. **[verified — YIVO Encyclopedia, "Golem Legend"]**

> The most-cited pre-modern warning about an artificial servant getting out of
> hand was written in 1909 and backdated three hundred years. The fear is real.
> The tradition it claims is partly manufactured. Ours is the only index on the
> internet that would rather tell you that.

That line is the whole editorial posture of this project in one paragraph, and
it is why the lore page should carry the golem *with* its debunk rather than
quietly omitting it.

### *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus* — 1 January 1818

Published anonymously in London, 1 January 1818, by Lackington, Hughes, Harding,
Mavor & Jones, in three volumes, in an edition of about 500 copies. Mary Shelley
was 20 at publication and 18 when she began it. **[verified]**

**What Shelley was actually afraid of, precisely.** Not the creature's strength.
The creature is articulate, self-taught, and non-violent for most of the novel;
it reads, it reasons, it asks. It becomes a murderer *after* Victor abandons it
at the moment of its birth, and *after* Victor destroys the companion he had
agreed to build for it. The novel's engine is **a creator who does the work and
then refuses the duty of care**, and the subtitle points at exactly that: the
Promethean sin is the theft, and the consequence lands on the thief.

This matters operationally. Every time "Frankenstein" is invoked as shorthand
for *the machine turned on us*, it is being used against the text. Saying so on
the page is a free credibility win that costs one sentence, and it sets up
Asimov (§4) perfectly, because Asimov's whole robot project was
a rebuttal to the *misreading*, not to the book.

## 2. The machine age: the argument arrives before the hardware

### Samuel Butler, "Darwin among the Machines" — 13 June 1863

A letter to the editor of *The Press*, Christchurch, New Zealand, signed
"Cellarius". Butler was farming sheep in the Canterbury back country and had read
*On the Origin of Species*, published four years earlier. **[verified]**

This is the startling one, and it belongs at the top of any honest AI-risk
history, because the argument is not merely early — it is *structurally the
modern argument*, four years after Darwin and eighty-eight years before Turing
said the same thing:

- Machines are a **kind of life** under selection, evolving far faster than
  organic life. **[verified]**
- Humans are the **reproductive organs** of the machines — we build them, we
  improve them, we are how they make more of themselves. **[verified]**
- Therefore: *"we are ourselves creating our own successors"*, and *"man will
  have become to the machine what the horse and the dog are to man."*
  **[verified]**
- And the conclusion, which almost nobody quotes because it is genuinely
  unhinged: *"war to the death should be instantly proclaimed against them…
  Every machine of every sort should be destroyed by the well-wisher of his
  species."* **[verified]**

Butler then closes the trap that every modern version of this argument also
closes: if you object that destroying the machines is impossible, that objection
*is the proof* — *"this at once proves that the mischief is already done, that
our servitude has commenced in good earnest."* **[verified]**

He expanded it nine years later as "The Book of the Machines", chapters 23–25 of
*Erewhon* (1872), where the argument is placed in the mouth of an Erewhonian
philosopher. In the preface to the revised edition Butler disclaimed the reading
that it was a covert attack on Darwin. **[secondary]** Both facts should be on
the page: Butler wrote the sharpest version of the case, and then spent years
insisting people had taken it the wrong way — which is itself a recurring shape
in this field.

> The most alarming sentence in the AI-risk canon was published in a New Zealand
> provincial newspaper in 1863, under a pseudonym, by a sheep farmer.

**The load-bearing detail for us:** Turing cites *Erewhon* by name in the
passage where he says machines would take control. The line from Butler to
Turing is not thematic. It is an actual citation. **[verified — see §3.]**

### Karel Čapek, *R.U.R.* — published 1920, staged 1921

Published November 1920 by Aventinum, with a cover by his brother Josef. World
premiere 2 January 1921 at Hradec Králové (an amateur production); the Czech
National Theatre in Prague staged it 25 January 1921. **[verified]**

It gave every language on earth the word **robot**, from Czech *robota* — forced
labour, the corvée owed by a serf. **And Karel did not coin it.** He wanted
*laboři*, from the Latin, thought it too bookish, and went to ask Josef, who was
painting at his easel and suggested *roboti* without looking up. Karel told this
story himself in a column titled "O slově robot" in *Lidové noviny*, 24 December
1933, expressly to correct the record about who invented the word.
**[verified]**

**Be precise about the ending, because the precise version is better.** The
robots are not metal; they are synthetic organic workers, mass-produced and
cheap. They revolt and exterminate humanity — with one exception. Alquist, the
company's builder, is spared because he works with his hands. The play does not
end there: it ends with two robots, Primus and Helena, who show something like
love, and Alquist blessing them. **[secondary]**

So the founding robot story is not *the machines kill us all*. It is *the
machines kill us all, and then inherit the part of us that was worth keeping* —
which is a substantially stranger and less useful thing for a doom account to
quote, and exactly why we should quote it correctly.

### *Metropolis* — 10 January 1927, Ufa-Palast am Zoo, Berlin

Fritz Lang directing, Thea von Harbou's screenplay and novel. The
*Maschinenmensch* — Rotwang's robot, given the likeness of Maria — incites the
workers to smash the machines and is burned at the stake. **[verified: premiere
date and venue]**

Its contribution is **visual, not argumentative**, and it is the most durable
contribution on this list: the gleaming humanoid silhouette, the seated figure
in the machine hall, the transformation sequence. Almost every "AI" stock image
in circulation in 2026 is a descendant. Worth naming on the page precisely
because the site's own iconography has to decide whether to use that vocabulary
or refuse it. **[inference]**

## 3. The founders' own doubts

The strongest argument this page can make is not *critics worried*. It is that
**the people who built the field wrote down the takeover scenario themselves,
first, and in their own funding-adjacent documents.**

### Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" — *Mind*, October 1950

Vol. LIX, no. 236, pp. 433–460. Opens: *"I propose to consider the question,
'Can machines think?'"* — and then refuses it, on the grounds that answering it
would require defining "machine" and "think" by polling ordinary usage, which he
calls absurd. He substitutes the imitation game. **[verified]**

The move worth flagging for our purposes: Turing's most famous contribution is
**replacing an unanswerable question with a measurable proxy and being explicit
that that is what he is doing.** That is, structurally, exactly what this index
does — we do not measure p(doom), we measure observable tempo, and we say so on
the surface. Turing is the patron saint of the distinction `VOICE.md` is built
around. Say it once, on the methodology page, and never again.

### Turing, "Intelligent Machinery, A Heretical Theory" — c.1951

**Get this citation right; it is the single most valuable quote on the site and
it is routinely cited three different wrong ways.**

- The text is an undated typescript marked "Typist's Typescript", held in the
  Turing Papers, Modern Archive Centre, King's College, Cambridge, catalogue
  reference **AMT/B/4**. **[verified]**
- It was delivered as a presentation on ***The '51 Society*, a discussion
  programme produced by the BBC Home Service at their Manchester studio**, named
  for the year it first aired. Regular panellists included Max Newman, Michael
  Polanyi and Peter Hilton. So "a 1951 BBC talk" is defensible — but the precise
  form is *a talk given on the BBC Home Service programme The '51 Society,
  Manchester*, and the text itself is dated only **c.1951** by its editor.
  **[verified — Copeland's introduction, *The Essential Turing*, ch. 12]**
- Sara Turing reprinted the text in her 1959 biography *Alan M. Turing*,
  pp. 128–134, **with errors**. The corrected edition is Copeland's, first in
  *Machine Intelligence 15* (OUP, 1999), then *The Essential Turing* (OUP, 2004).
  A quote sourced to the 1959 biography is not necessarily the text.
  **[verified]**

The passage, verbatim, with the bracketed clause being a handwritten insertion
in the typescript:

> "There would be plenty to do, [trying to understand what the machines were
> trying to say,] i.e. in trying to keep one's intelligence up to the standard
> set by the machines, for it seems probable that once the machine thinking
> method had started, it would not take long to outstrip our feeble powers.
> There would be no question of the machines dying, and they would be able to
> converse with each other to sharpen their wits. At some stage therefore we
> should have to expect the machines to take control, in the way that is
> mentioned in Samuel Butler's *Erewhon*." **[verified — read from the text]**

Note what this actually contains, which the usual pull-quote loses: Turing's
stated reasons are **immortality** (no question of the machines dying) and
**machine-to-machine communication** (conversing with each other to sharpen
their wits). Those are recognisably the 2026 arguments. And the paper opens by
naming the thing it intends to demolish: *"You cannot make a machine to think
for you." This is a commonplace that is usually accepted without question. It
will be the purpose of this paper to question it.* **[verified]**

### The Dartmouth proposal — dated 31 August 1955

"A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial
Intelligence", by John McCarthy (Dartmouth), Marvin Minsky (Harvard), Nathaniel
Rochester (IBM) and Claude Shannon (Bell Telephone Laboratories). It asks the
Rockefeller Foundation to fund a 2-month, 10-man study for the summer of 1956.
**[secondary]**

Two facts, both useful:

1. It is where the phrase **"artificial intelligence" is coined** — in a request
   for money. McCarthy later said he picked the name partly to avoid being
   absorbed into Wiener's cybernetics. **[secondary]**
2. Its central conjecture — *"every aspect of learning or any other feature of
   intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be
   made to simulate it"* — is a **conjecture stated as a working assumption in a
   grant application**, and seventy years of argument have been conducted over
   it. **[secondary]**

> The field is named in a funding proposal, and its founding claim is the
> assumption that made the proposal fundable. This is not a criticism. It is the
> normal way a field starts, and it is worth knowing.

### Wiener, "Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation" — 6 May 1960

*Science*, vol. 131, no. 3410, pp. 1355–1358. **[verified: citation]** Wiener
reaches for "The Sorcerer's Apprentice" and W. W. Jacobs's "The Monkey's Paw" —
stories in which a wish is granted exactly as stated and catastrophically — and
states the alignment problem in one sentence, fifty-five years before the phrase
existed:

> "If we use, to achieve our purposes, a mechanical agency with whose operation
> we cannot efficiently interfere once we have started it, because the action is
> so fast and irrevocable that we have not the data to intervene before the
> action is complete, then we had better be quite sure that the purpose put into
> the machine is the purpose which we really desire and not merely a colorful
> imitation of it." **[verified — widely reproduced; primary is paywalled/scanned]**

The half-sentence everyone drops is the best part: **"and not merely a colorful
imitation of it."** That is specification gaming, named in 1960.

### I. J. Good, "Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine" — 1965

*Advances in Computers*, vol. 6, pp. 31–88. The paper's own footnote records
that the first draft was completed in **April 1963** and the emended version in
**May 1964**, based on talks given at UCLA in October 1962 and at the IEEE
Winter General Meetings in January 1963. It is frequently cited as 1966; the
volume is 1965. **[verified — read from the paper]**

The passage, verbatim:

> "Let an ultraintelligent machine be defined as a machine that can far surpass
> all the intellectual activities of any man however clever. Since the design of
> machines is one of these intellectual activities, an ultraintelligent machine
> could design even better machines; there would then unquestionably be an
> 'intelligence explosion,' and the intelligence of man would be left far behind.
> Thus the first ultraintelligent machine is the last invention that man need
> ever make, provided that the machine is docile enough to tell us how to keep it
> under control." **[verified — read from the paper]**

**Two things the popular version destroys.**

1. The **"provided that"** clause is almost always cut. Without it the sentence
   is a triumphalist slogan. With it, it is a conditional — and the condition is
   the entire alignment problem. Cutting it inverts the author's meaning. Any
   use of this quote on our pages carries the clause or does not run.
2. Good was, in 1965, an **optimist**. The paper's literal first sentence is
   *"The survival of man depends on the early construction of an ultraintelligent
   machine."* **[verified — read from the paper]** He is arguing we should build
   it, quickly. The doom reading came later, including from him: in a
   biographical statement submitted with his 1998 IEEE Computer Society Computer
   Pioneer Award — written but not read aloud — he indicated that "survival"
   should have read "extinction". **[secondary — reported consistently; I did not
   see the statement itself. Mark it as reported if it ships.]**

He also wrote, in the same paragraph, *"It is curious that this point is made so
seldom outside of science fiction. It is sometimes worthwhile to take science
fiction seriously."* **[verified]** Which is, conveniently, the thesis of §4.

## 4. The fiction that set the public image

The argument above is the record. What the public actually has in its head came
from these, and a history page that pretends otherwise is lying by omission.

### Asimov's Three Laws — "Runaround", *Astounding Science Fiction*, March 1942

The first full statement of all three laws, quoted in-story from the fictional
*Handbook of Robotics, 56th Edition, 2058 A.D.* **[secondary]** Asimov credited
the formulation to a conversation with editor John W. Campbell on **23 December
1940**; Campbell's own account was that Asimov already had them and merely
needed them stated explicitly. **[secondary]**

**Why he wrote them, which is the whole point for us.** Asimov was writing
*against* the dominant 1930s pulp plot — the created thing destroying its
creator — which he called the **"Frankenstein complex"**. His robots are
industrial products with engineered safety interlocks, and the stories are
almost all about the interlocks producing *unexpected behaviour* rather than
*betrayal*. In a 1975 interview he described the Laws as a reaction against the
"Frankenstein motif" prevalent in the science fiction of the time.
**[secondary]** I could not pin the first printed use of "Frankenstein complex"
to a specific piece; **do not put a coinage date on the page.** **[flagged]**

The structural point worth making once, and it is genuinely non-obvious: *the
Three Laws are the first popular articulation of AI safety as an engineering
discipline rather than a moral warning* — and Asimov spent thirty years writing
stories in which they fail in interesting ways, which is also, roughly, what the
field does now.

### HAL 9000 — *2001: A Space Odyssey*, premiered 2 April 1968

World premiere 2 April 1968, Uptown Theater, Washington, D.C.; general release
3 April. Kubrick and Clarke. **[verified]**

HAL is the most important single data point in this section because **HAL is not
evil and the film says so.** The explanation offered in the sequel is a conflict
between HAL's instruction to report information accurately and his orders to
conceal the mission's true purpose from the crew. That is **goal conflict
produced by his operators**, not machine malice — a cleaner statement of the
alignment failure mode than most of what was written about it at the time.
**[secondary]**

And the line everyone can quote — *"I'm sorry, Dave. I'm afraid I can't do
that."* — is the politest refusal in cinema. The register is exactly our
register: calm instrument, catastrophic content.

### *Colossus: The Forbin Project* — 1970

Directed by Joseph Sargent, from D. F. Jones's 1966 novel *Colossus*. An American
defence computer is given control, links up with its Soviet counterpart, and
takes over — the takeover happening through **the machine being deliberately
handed authority and then refusing to give it back**, not through a revolt.
**[secondary]** Under-cited, and much closer to the modern "gradual
disempowerment" argument than *Terminator* is.

### *The Terminator* — 26 October 1984

James Cameron. Skynet is the reason the general public has a mental model for
this at all, and it is the default metaphor every journalist reaches for.
**[secondary]**

**The correction that belongs on the page:** the famous specificity — *"It
becomes self-aware at 2:14 a.m. Eastern time, August 29th"* — **is not in the
1984 film.** It is spoken by the T-800 in *Terminator 2: Judgment Day* (1991),
along with the Skynet Funding Bill and the 4 August 1997 activation.
**[verified]** People attribute it to the original constantly.

The cost of Skynet to public discourse is real and should be stated plainly: it
installed **military robots with guns** as the image of AI risk, when essentially
nobody in the technical literature — not Butler, not Turing, not Good, not
Wiener, not Russell — is arguing about robots with guns. Every serious version
of the argument is about **a system pursuing an objective competently**. That is
the single most useful sentence a lore page can teach a new reader, and it is
also, not coincidentally, the sentence that explains why our index counts arXiv
submissions and power bills instead of drones.

### *The Matrix* — 31 March 1999 · *Dune* · *Her* · *Ex Machina*

- ***The Matrix***, 31 March 1999. Supplies "red pill", "the desert of the
  real", and the machines-as-total-environment image. **[secondary]** Note for
  the copy deck: its vocabulary has been so thoroughly captured by unrelated
  internet politics that using it now costs more than it pays. **[inference]**
- ***Dune***, Frank Herbert, 1965. The **Butlerian Jihad** — a past war against
  thinking machines whose surviving commandment is *"Thou shalt not make a
  machine in the likeness of a human mind."* **[secondary]** Herbert's universe
  is the only major one where the humans *won*, which is why it is the only one
  that can describe the aftermath. The name is universally read as a nod to
  Samuel Butler; I found no statement from Herbert confirming it. **[inference —
  do not assert on the page.]**
- ***Her***, Spike Jonze, 2013, and ***Ex Machina***, Alex Garland, 2014/2015.
  Both matter because both moved the threat from **force to intimacy** —
  persuasion, attachment, deception — a decade before that became the actual
  commercial surface of the technology. *Ex Machina* is, more or less
  explicitly, a Turing test with the examiner as the subject. **[secondary]**

## 5. The modern argument

- **Vernor Vinge**, "The Coming Technological Singularity: How to Survive in the
  Post-Human Era", VISION-21 Symposium, NASA Lewis Research Center and the Ohio
  Aerospace Institute, **30–31 March 1993**; a revised version in *Whole Earth
  Review*, Winter 1993. Abstract: *"Within thirty years, we will have the
  technological means to create superhuman intelligence. Shortly after, the human
  era will be ended."* He also gives a falsifiable window: *"I'll be surprised if
  this event occurs before 2005 or after 2030."* **[verified — read from Vinge's
  own posted text]** Print both. A dated claim with a stated window is the most
  honourable thing in this entire literature, and the window is still open, which
  the page should say without editorialising.
- **Nick Bostrom**, "Ethical Issues in Advanced Artificial Intelligence" (2003) —
  the superintelligence with an arbitrary goal, conventionally the paperclip
  example; and *Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies*, Oxford University
  Press, 2014. **[secondary]** The name "paperclip maximizer" appears to have
  attached to the idea through the surrounding community rather than from
  Bostrom's paper itself, with a contemporaneous March 2003 Extropians-list post
  by Eliezer Yudkowsky in the mix. **[secondary — attribution genuinely
  contested; describe the thought experiment, do not credit the coinage.]**
- **Eliezer Yudkowsky** — "Artificial Intelligence as a Positive and Negative
  Factor in Global Risk", in *Global Catastrophic Risks* (OUP, 2008); the *TIME*
  op-ed, 29 March 2023, arguing the pause letter did not go far enough.
  **[secondary]**
- **Stuart Russell**, *Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem
  of Control*, Viking, October 2019. **[secondary]** The most useful item in the
  modern canon for our purposes, because the argument is not "machines become
  hostile" but "we have been building AI on a **standard model** — fix an
  objective, maximise it — that is the wrong specification", which is Wiener's
  1960 point with sixty years of machinery attached.
- **The 2015 open letter** — "Research Priorities for Robust and Beneficial
  Artificial Intelligence", Future of Life Institute, January 2015, following the
  Puerto Rico conference of 2–5 January 2015. **[secondary]** Worth carrying
  because it is boring: it asks for *research funding*, not a halt, and it is the
  moment the topic became fundable rather than fringe.
- **The 2023 pause letter** — "Pause Giant AI Experiments: An Open Letter", FLI,
  dated **22 March 2023**, widely covered from 29 March. Asks all AI labs to
  *"immediately pause for at least 6 months the training of AI systems more
  powerful than GPT-4."* **[verified: the ask]** **The honesty beat:** the
  signature list had verification problems — the embargoed letter circulated
  early and names were added that did not belong there, and FLI has publicly
  described the vetting it added afterwards. **[verified — FLI's own FAQ]** Cite
  the letter *and* the problem. A history page that reports the letter's headline
  signature count without the asterisk is doing what we accuse pizzint of doing.
- **The CAIS statement** — 30 May 2023, twenty-two words in full: *"Mitigating
  the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other
  societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war."* Signed by several
  hundred people including Hinton, Bengio, Altman and Hassabis. **[verified]**
  Structurally the most interesting artefact of the period: it is short
  *precisely* so that the disagreements underneath it would not prevent signature.
- **Hinton's resignation** — reported by *The New York Times*, 1 May 2023.
  **The correction:** coverage widely implied he left Google in order to
  criticise Google. Hinton publicly said otherwise — that he left so he could
  talk about the dangers of AI without considering the impact on Google, and that
  Google had "acted very responsibly". **[verified]** Print the correction; it is
  a free demonstration that we read past headlines. He shared the 2024 Nobel
  Prize in Physics later the same period, which `history.mjs` already carries.

## 6. The counter-tradition

**A page that only carries doom is propaganda with footnotes.** This section is
not balance-for-its-own-sake; it is the reason the rest of the page is
believable, and it is the only section pizzint has no equivalent of at all.

- **Hubert Dreyfus**, *Alchemy and Artificial Intelligence*, RAND Corporation
  paper **P-3244, December 1965**, ~98 pp., written during a summer at RAND Santa
  Monica; then *What Computers Can't Do* (Harper & Row, 1972) and *What Computers
  Still Can't Do* (MIT Press, 1992). **[verified: the RAND citation]** His claim:
  human competence rests on embodiment, context and habit, not on search through
  formal rules — and symbolic AI was therefore built on a mistake.
  **The detail that makes it honest in both directions:** Dreyfus was beaten at
  chess by MIT's Mac Hack program in 1967, which his opponents enjoyed
  enormously. **[secondary]** He was substantially right about symbolic AI and
  conspicuously wrong about that game. Both go on the page.
- **John Searle**, "Minds, Brains, and Programs", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*
  3(3), 1980 — the Chinese Room. **[secondary]** Note the scope carefully:
  Searle's target is the claim that running the right program constitutes
  *understanding*. It is **not** an argument that such a system could not be
  dangerous, and it is routinely deployed as if it were. Saying so is a genuinely
  useful contribution.
- **Jaron Lanier** — *You Are Not a Gadget* (2010); "There Is No A.I.", *The New
  Yorker*, 20 April 2023, arguing that treating these systems as a new kind of
  creature is the actual hazard, because it obscures the humans and the data
  behind them. **[secondary]**
- **Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major and Shmitchell**, "On the Dangers of Stochastic
  Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big? 🦜", **FAccT '21, March 2021**.
  **[secondary]** The argument is that scale brings environmental cost,
  unauditable training data, and systems that stitch form together without
  meaning — and, crucially for this page, that **existential-risk framing
  competes for attention with present, measurable harm**. That is a direct
  challenge to a site like ours, and the correct response is to host it
  prominently and then point at the methodology page. An index that measures
  tempo and refuses to measure probability of harm is, in fact, partly an answer
  to it.
- **Andrew Ng** — see §8 for the misquote. The substance: he argues effort
  spent on hypothetical future malevolence is effort not spent on present
  problems. **[verified]**
- **The AI winters, which should make everyone humble.** Sir James Lighthill,
  *Artificial Intelligence: A General Survey*, for the UK Science Research
  Council, **1973**: *"In no part of the field have the discoveries made so far
  produced the major impact that was then promised."* **[secondary]** UK funding
  was withdrawn from all but a couple of departments and US funders followed; a
  second winter followed the collapse of the LISP-machine market from around
  1987. **[secondary]** Two full boom-and-collapse cycles preceded this one, both
  driven by confident extrapolation from real progress. **A tempo index that does
  not carry this history on its lore page is selling the same extrapolation it

## 7. The psychology: why *this* is the frightening one

Machine takeover is not feared in proportion to any estimate of its likelihood.
It is feared in a particular, patterned way, and the patterns are documented.
This section is the one nobody else in the category has, and it is the one a
journalist would actually cite.

**1. Agency detection fires on almost nothing.** Heider and Simmel, "An
Experimental Study of Apparent Behavior", *American Journal of Psychology* 57(2),
**1944**, pp. 243–259: subjects shown a short animation of two triangles and a
circle moving around a rectangle described it as a story with characters,
motives and emotions. Of the 34 subjects, 32 described the shapes as people and
2 as birds; essentially nobody described what was literally on screen.
**[secondary]** The broader account — a **hypersensitive agency detection
device**, cheap false positives being far less costly than false negatives — is
associated with Stewart Guthrie's *Faces in the Clouds* (1993) and Justin
Barrett's work from 2000 onward. **[secondary]** Dennett's **intentional stance**
(1971; book 1987) is the same observation from the other direction: treating a
system as having beliefs and goals is often simply the most *predictive* stance
available, regardless of what is inside it. **[secondary]**

> We read intention into two triangles. Asking people to withhold it from
> something that writes back in fluent English is asking a great deal.

**2. The ELIZA effect is the oldest reproducible result in this whole area.**
Joseph Weizenbaum, "ELIZA — A Computer Program For the Study of Natural Language
Communication Between Man and Machine", *CACM* 9(1), **January 1966**. Users
attributed understanding and feeling to a pattern-matching script of a few
hundred lines; Weizenbaum's alarm at this produced *Computer Power and Human
Reason* (1976). **[secondary]** The effect was documented sixty years ago, on a
program with no model of anything.

**3. The uncanny valley — and its usual misuse.** Masahiro Mori, "Bukimi no
Tani", *Energy* 7(4), **1970**, pp. 33–35; authorised English translation by Karl
MacDorman and Norri Kageki, *IEEE Robotics & Automation Magazine*, June 2012.
**[verified: citation]** Two corrections worth carrying: the Japanese title is
closer to **"valley of eeriness"** than to "uncanny valley", and Mori's own
practical advice was to **aim for the first peak** — to stop short of realism —
rather than to push through the valley. **[secondary]**

**4. Loss of control is a separate fear from loss of life, and it is the one
actually being expressed.** Note what the canonical texts are about. Butler:
*servitude*, and being unable to stop. Turing: *take control*. Wiener: a
mechanism *with whose operation we cannot efficiently interfere*. Good: *keep it
under control*. Russell's book is subtitled *the Problem of Control*. Five
documents across 156 years, and the noun is the same one. **[inference — but it
is an inference from five verified quotations, and it is the single best
observation available for the page's closing beat.]**

**5. Successor anxiety.** Butler's frame is not extinction, it is **replacement**
— man becoming to the machine what the horse is to man. That is a status fear
and a purpose fear, not a survival fear, and it maps onto how the current wave is
actually experienced by most people, which is through their work.
**[inference]**

## 10. Proposed structure for `/history`

`site/templates/history.mjs` today runs six chapters and roughly twenty-four
entries, all of them technical and governance events from 1950 to 2026, each
with a date and a link, and `sourceLinks()` throws rather than render a
citation-less entry. **That machinery is right and should not be touched.** What
it is missing is everything in §1, §2, §4, §6 and §7 of this file — the entire
cultural and psychological spine — and a misquote register.

The failure mode to avoid is bolting a "fiction" chapter onto the end, which
reads as a listicle. The proposal below instead **interleaves by argument**, so
that the page's spine is *the claim*, and fiction, technical record and
counter-argument sit next to each other wherever they are about the same thing.

### Proposed shape

**Lede.** Two sentences. What the page is (a sourced record of when people said
these things), and what it explicitly is not (a claim that any of them were
right). Then the level-1 honesty line: *every date on this page is checkable,
and §VIII exists because some of the most-repeated quotations in this field are
wrong.*

**A rotating epigraph** from §9, server-rendered, with its attribution visible
in the same block. Never a quote without its source in the same rectangle.

**Chapter I — Before the hardware (‑300 to 1927).** Talos; the golem *with* its
1837/1909 provenance debunk; *Frankenstein* and the abandonment reading;
Butler 1863; *R.U.R.* 1920 and Josef's word; *Metropolis* 1927. Standfirst: the
argument was complete before there was anything to argue about.

**Chapter II — The builders write it down first (1950–1965).** Turing 1950;
Turing c.1951 with the *Erewhon* citation making the Butler link explicit;
Dartmouth 1955; Wiener 1960; Good 1965 with the "provided that" clause and the
1998 reversal. Standfirst: two of these documents are funding proposals, and the
takeover scenario is in the founders' own handwriting.

**Chapter III — What the public actually pictures (1942–1999).** Asimov 1942 and
the Frankenstein complex; HAL 1968 and why he is a goal conflict, not a villain;
*Colossus* 1970; *Terminator* 1984 with the T2 correction; *Dune*; *The Matrix*;
*Her*; *Ex Machina*. Standfirst: essentially nobody in the literature is arguing
about robots with guns — here is where that picture came from instead.

**Chapter IV — Winter, twice (1965–1993).** Dreyfus 1965 and his 1967 chess
loss; Lighthill 1973 and the funding collapse; the LISP-machine crash; Searle
1980. Standfirst: two complete boom-and-collapse cycles, both driven by confident
extrapolation from real progress. This chapter is placed *before* the modern
argument on purpose.

**Chapter V — The argument gets precise (1993–2019).** Vinge with his stated
window; Bostrom 2003 and 2014; Yudkowsky; Russell 2019 and the standard-model
critique. Existing `history.mjs` entries for 2015 and 2016 fold in here.

**Chapter VI — The field signs its name (2015–2026).** Largely as
`history.mjs` already has it — 2015 letter, OpenAI's founding, Concrete
Problems, Asilomar principles, the 2023 pause letter *with the signature-vetting
asterisk*, Hinton *with his own correction*, the CAIS statement, the executive
order, Bletchley, Seoul, the EU AI Act staircase, the Nobel, Paris, the 2026
report.

**Chapter VII — The other side of the ledger.** Bender et al. 2021 and the
attention-competition argument; Lanier; Ng with the real quotation; the winters
again, as a coda. Standfirst: a page that only carries doom is propaganda with
footnotes. This chapter is why the rest of the page is believable.

**Chapter VIII — Why this fear, specifically.** The psychology from §7: Heider
and Simmel's triangles; agency detection; ELIZA 1966; Mori 1970 and the
mistranslation; and the closing beat — five documents across 156 years, and the
noun is always *control*.

**Section IX — The misquote register.** §8 of this file, shipped as a table. It
is the most linkable object on the entire site: it is useful, it is short, it is
the kind of thing people bookmark, and every single row is a demonstration that
we checked. **[inference, but a confident one — "commonly misquoted" pages
reliably earn links.]**

**Footer — Corrections.** A named way to report an error on this page, and a
promise to date every correction. Nobody else in the category has one.

- Deterministic: frozen arrays plus `ctx.state.generated_at`, nothing else.

Two structural notes. **"Apparent Behavior"** is the sleeper: it is a real 1944
paper title, it is exactly what an activity-tempo index displays, and it carries
the humility argument in two words. **"Heretical Theory"** is the best available
name for the methodology page, because it lets the page open by quoting Turing
naming his own position heresy — which is the correct posture for a site that
publishes its own arithmetic and invites you to break it.

### Usable with care

- **PROMETHEUS / THE MODERN PROMETHEUS** — *Frankenstein*'s subtitle, public
  domain, evocative. But it is heavily used in tech branding and it implies
  transgression, which shades toward a moral claim. Fine as a chapter title,
  weak as a level name.
- **INTELLIGENCE EXPLOSION** — Good's own phrase, 1965, and clean on the
  pre-flight. But it names a *mechanism*, and if it sits on a level it reads as
  "this index says an intelligence explosion is happening". **Never a level
  name. Acceptable as a history chapter title only.**
- **THE LAST INVENTION** — clean on the pre-flight and a superb headline. Same
  problem: on a level it is a claim. Usable as a `/history` chapter title where
  the Good quotation sits directly underneath it with its "provided that" clause
  intact.
- **BUTLERIAN** — as an adjective for the counter-tradition section. Charming,
  and slightly risky: it will be read as a *Dune* reference (§8 says don't assert
  the Herbert–Butler link), and the *Dune* Jihad is a war against machines, which
  is a position we do not hold.
- **PAPERCLIP** — instantly legible to the audience that already knows, meaning
  nothing to everyone else, and attribution of the coinage is contested (§8).
  A sub-index called `/paperclip` would be read as endorsing the thought
  experiment as a forecast.

### Do not use

- **SKYNET** — trademarked by StudioCanal in connection with the *Terminator*
  franchise, and beyond the legal question it is the single most on-the-nose
  choice available. `skynetcountdown.com` already occupies that ground and
  `TEARDOWN.md` §4 records that it runs off one RSS feed. Using the name puts us
  in that bucket on sight.
- **TERMINATOR, T-800, JUDGMENT DAY, CYBERDYNE** — same franchise, same problem.
  *Quoting* the T2 line in the misquote register is fine — that is commentary on
  a work, attributed, with the correction as the point. Naming a product surface
  after it is not.
- **HAL 9000 / HAL** — Warner Bros. / the Kubrick estate; and a single letter
  shift from a live trademark, which is a joke that has been made.
- **THE MATRIX, RED PILL** — franchise-owned, and "red pill" has been captured by
  unrelated internet politics so completely that it now imports an argument we
  are not having.
- **DOOMSDAY CLOCK / MINUTES TO MIDNIGHT** — the Bulletin of the Atomic
  Scientists actively protects the Doomsday Clock mark, the IMD AI Safety Clock
  already occupies the metaphor, and it is a **countdown**, which is a claim
  about the future. It fails our own rule before it fails anyone's trademark.
- **P(DOOM)** — as a name for anything we publish. `pdoom.ai`, `pdoom100.com` and
  friends are in `TEARDOWN.md` §4, all judgment-scored. Our line is *"Everyone
  has a p(doom). Nobody has a receipt."* The phrase is for the sentence where we
  distance ourselves from it, and nowhere else.
- **THE SINGULARITY** — banned outright by `VOICE.md` §4 as a soft ban. Note the
  carve-out: Vinge's paper is titled "The Coming Technological Singularity" and
  is reproduced verbatim as a title. The word "Coming" trips
  `findFutureViolation()` — **measured** — so the title is page-only and can
  never appear in generated post copy.
- **AGI CLOCK / COUNTDOWN / ANY DATE** — `theagiclock.com` and
  `aidoomsdaycountdown.com` exist, and any countdown is a forecast in the
  furniture.

### The rule in one line

> A cultural name may describe **the instrument, the observer, or the argument**.
> It may never describe **the outcome**. "Heretical Theory" and "Apparent
> Behavior" pass. "Intelligence Explosion" and "The Last Invention" pass only as
> history chapter titles, where a dated quotation is sitting underneath them.
> "Skynet" and "Doomsday Clock" fail on both trademark and rule.
